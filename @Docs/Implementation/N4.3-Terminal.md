# N4.3 — The terminal: RFC 0020's console over a device

The third slice of the application environment (RFC 0015 §8: "the terminal of RFC 0020 later"; Strategy §60's
System.Console and headless requirements, RFC 0020 §6 and §8). A process whose standard streams are the terminal reads
what a person types and writes what a person sees; `Console.ReadLine`, `Console.ReadKey`, `Console.CancelKeyPress`
and `Console.IsInputRedirected == false` work as on Linux. The step has two parts: the device, its driver and the
terminal service (N4.3a), and the libc's standard streams over the service with the checks (N4.3b).

## What .NET needs

System.Native's `pal_console.c` is the whole surface. `SystemNative_InitializeTerminalAndSignalHandling` reads the
terminal's attributes with `tcgetattr(0)`; when that fails there is no terminal and `Console.IsInputRedirected` is
true (today). Before a read, `SystemNative_InitializeConsoleBeforeRead` sets the attributes with `tcsetattr(0,
TCSANOW)`: `ISIG` on or off as `Console.TreatControlCAsInput` says, `ECHO`, `ICANON`, `IEXTEN`, `IXON`, `IXOFF`,
`ICRNL`, `INLCR` and `IGNCR` off, `VMIN` and `VTIME` as the caller asks (one byte, no timeout for `ReadKey`); .NET
does its own echo and line editing in that raw mode. `SystemNative_ReadStdin` is `read(0)`, `SystemNative_StdinReady`
is `poll(0, POLLIN, 0)`, `SystemNative_GetWindowSize` is `ioctl(TIOCGWINSZ)`, `SystemNative_IsATty` is `isatty`, and
`SystemNative_GetControlCharacters` reads the `c_cc` array. The signal thread installs `SIGINT`, `SIGQUIT`, `SIGCHLD`,
`SIGCONT`, `SIGWINCH`, `SIGTTOU` and the rest (R4.1); `Console.CancelKeyPress` runs when `SIGINT` arrives, and an
unhandled `SIGINT` ends the process by its default action. `UninitializeTerminal` restores the attributes at exit.

WitOS has the output half (the kernel log, R4.1) and the signals' dispositions (S3), but no input, no attributes and
no device a person types into. The kernel log stays what RFC 0011 §7.3 says it is: the kernel's last-resort output,
not the terminal.

## Decisions

**The device.** A virtio console (virtio 1.x §5.3, type 3) on the PCI bus of both boards, which QEMU gives as
`virtio-serial-pci` with one port (`max_ports=1`, so that the device offers no `MULTIPORT` and has two queues: 0
receives, 1 transmits) and a `virtconsole` on a character device the runner owns. The kernel log keeps the board's
UART; the terminal is a second device the platform enumerates like the disks and the entropy source, and the kernel
knows nothing of it (RFC 0011 §7.7). The device manager already names the type `SerialConsole`; its driver table
maps it to `/system/Drivers/VirtioConsole`.

**The runner.** `BootRequest.Terminal` adds the device, as `DataDisk` adds a disk. As the QMP control does, the runner
owns a loopback listener before QEMU starts and QEMU's chardev connects to it (`-chardev socket,...,server=off`), so
there is no port race on either host; the runner records everything the guest wrote in `<name>.terminal.log` beside
the serial log and types what the request's script says: each `TerminalInput(After, Text)` sends its bytes once the
line `After` appeared in the terminal's output. The judge looks for a scenario's required lines in the serial log and
the terminal log alike, since the output of a process with a terminal goes to the terminal, not to the kernel log.

**The driver** (`src/Services/Drivers/VirtioConsole`, a NativeAOT program as the block driver is) takes the device
from the device manager (`witos/driver.h`), opens the modern PCI transport, negotiates `VIRTIO_F_VERSION_1` with
nothing else (`VIRTIO_CONSOLE_F_SIZE` is taken when offered, for the size; QEMU offers it not), sets up both queues on
pinned pages of its own, keeps the receive queue full of buffers and serves a byte-stream protocol (`witos/serial.h`)
on the channel its ready answer offers: `ATTACH` moves the client's window, `WRITE` transmits bytes of the window and
answers when the device took them, `READ` answers with the bytes received, at least one, when there are any, holding
the one request until they come, and `SIZE` answers the device's columns and rows or zeros. One client, one request
of each kind at a time; the driver waits on its channel and its interrupt with one `OBJECT_WAIT`. The transport and
the ring code the block driver wrote move to `OutWit.OS/Hardware/Virtio` (`VirtioPciTransport`, `Virtqueue`) and both
drivers compile them, since a second driver is what a shared source is for; the block driver's behaviour does not
change, and the device scenarios prove it.

**The service** (`src/Services/Terminal`, `/system/Terminal`, `witos/terminal.h`) is started by the device manager
after the namespace service, given the console driver's channel in a `WitTerminalDevice` message, and answers ready
with its clients' endpoint (`SEND`, `DUPLICATE`, `TRANSFER`). It owns the line discipline, which the libc never does:
the attributes (a platform-neutral mirror of `termios`: the four flag words and the control characters), canonical
mode with echo, erase and kill, a line delivered whole, the end of the file on `VEOF`; raw mode with `VMIN` and
`VTIME`; `ISIG` turning `VINTR` and `VQUIT` into signals; `OPOST` with `ONLCR` for output, since a terminal wants
`\r\n`; and the size, 80 by 24 unless the device reports one. A client connects once (`CONNECT`: the end of a session
channel, the end of an events channel, a window of its own) and sends one request at a time on the session: `READ`
(blocking per the discipline, or `NONBLOCK`), `WRITE`, `GET_ATTRIBUTES`, `SET_ATTRIBUTES` (now, drain or flush),
`GET_SIZE`, `POLL` (what a `poll` would report now), `FLUSH` and `DRAIN`. Events go the other way on the events
channel, unsolicited: `INPUT` (bytes arrived), `SIGNAL` (`SIGINT`, `SIGQUIT`), `SIZE` and `HANGUP`; a signal
character also answers every pending `READ` with `EINTR`, as Linux's reads return. Every process that connects shares
the one terminal: the input goes to whoever reads first, a signal to every session, as a Linux session without job
control sees them. Each session is a thread of the service, as the namespace service serves its sessions.

**How a process gets it.** The device manager's ready answer carries the namespace's clients' endpoint today; the
status (`WitServiceStatus` version 5) says in `Capabilities` which endpoints follow in its handle slots, the namespace's
then the terminal's, and the process manager keeps both and gives every process it starts afterwards each one that is
there (`WIT_START_TERMINAL`, after the namespace in the order of the bits). The first start message holds what fits in
four handles; `WIT_START_MORE` (N4.2's `WIT_START_NAMESPACE_NEXT`, generalized before it ships) says a second message
on the start endpoint carries the rest in the same order. The services the device manager starts, and the device
manager itself, have no terminal: they start before it exists, and their diagnostics stay on the kernel log, which the
boot gates read.

**The libc** (N4.3b). The standard descriptors keep the form S6.2 gave them: slots that stand for the standard
streams until closed. With a terminal in the process context (`WitProcessContext.Terminal`), a standard stream is the
terminal: `read` of 0 is a `READ` of the session, `write` of 1 and 2 a `WRITE`, `TCGETS`, `TCSETS`, `TCSETSW`,
`TCSETSF`, `TIOCGWINSZ`, `TCFLSH` and `FIONREAD` the matching requests, so that `isatty` is true for all three,
`tcgetattr` and `tcsetattr` work and `poll` reports the terminal's readiness through `POLL`; without one, the streams
stay the log and the empty input (R4.1), unchanged. The session is made at the first use, and with it the terminal
thread, which waits on the events endpoint and raises each `SIGNAL` on itself with `tkill` (the handler runs there, as
`SIGCHLD`'s does on the reaper, N4.2) and bumps the poll word on `INPUT` so that a parked `poll` looks again. A
duplicate of a standard stream (`dup`, a child's `adddup2(1, 2)`) is the terminal too; a child inherits the terminal
through its start message, not through its descriptors, and `posix_spawn`'s closed streams keep their meaning.
Nothing enters the libc's namespace: the session lives in `src/Substrate/libc/terminal.c` behind `__wit_` names.

**Checks.** N4.3a: the `runtime-terminal` scenario boots the device services with the terminal device, the device
manager reports the console driver and the terminal service ready, and the service answers a line the runner types
(`[TERMINAL] echo: …`) through the driver before `/bin/init` runs — the service's own loopback, which proves the
device, the driver and the discipline without the libc. N4.3b: `tests/User/terminal_init.c` as `/bin/init` of the same
scenario reads a line the runner types in canonical mode and prints it back, reads one key in raw mode, requires
`isatty` of 0–2, a real `TIOCGWINSZ`, `tcgetattr`/`tcsetattr` round trips and `SIGINT` from a typed Ctrl-C through a
handler, with the end of the file on Ctrl-D; and the compatibility suite gains a `Terminal` area, run when its input
is a terminal and reported as not applicable otherwise (the hosts' test runs it without one): `Console.ReadLine`,
`Console.ReadKey`, `Console.KeyAvailable`, `Console.IsInputRedirected == false`, `Console.WindowWidth`,
`Console.CancelKeyPress` and `PosixSignalRegistration` for `SIGINT`, driven by the runner's script in
`runtime-compatibility`, which boots with the terminal from then on.

**Which channel a probe takes.** The device manager's storage probe ran over every driver that offered a service
channel, so on the first guest run the console driver's serial channel got a block `INFO`, answered in the serial
protocol ("a block reply of 32 bytes is not of version 1"), the probe closed the channel and the terminal service
could not be started with it (`CHANNEL_SEND`, `EBADF`). The block probe now goes to the drivers of the
`BlockStorageController` class alone; a driver's class, not the presence of a service channel, says which protocol
the channel speaks.

**A prompt after the read.** The scenario's `/bin/init` prints "press Ctrl-C" and reads; the runner types the moment
the line appears, and on ARM64 the `^C` reached the service before the read did, so the signal went to the handler
and the read waited for a line that never came — as on Linux, where only a read that is waiting ends with `EINTR`.
The prompts that ask for a signal character or the end of the file are printed from a thread of their own a quarter
of a second after the read began, through the control session while the read holds the reader's. The suite's
`CancelKeyPress` and `PosixSignalRegistration` checks wait for the handler alone and need no pending read.

**A line of its own for the console.** On q35 the kernel takes a function's PIC line from its Interrupt Line
register, which the firmware fills from the LPC bridge's PIRQ routing — OVMF routes the PIRQs to two lines, 10 and
11, so with two block devices and the console a third virtio device shared a bound line, and `INTERRUPT_BIND`
answered `BUSY` (the kernel binds a line once, K3.2). The q35 platform now routes the PIRQs the root bus's slots
assert to four lines of its own (10, 11, 5, 6; marked level-triggered in the ELCR) and gives every root-bus
function below the chipset's slots the line of its pin and slot by q35's rotation, after checking that the rotation
reproduces every line the firmware assigned from its own routing; the registers are written back so that they stay
truthful, and the kernel logs the routing (`PCI INTx:` lines) so that a board's lines are measured. Two facts of
QEMU's ICH9 cost a round each: an ordinary slot's pins assert PIRQ E–H (`(slot + pin) % 4 + 4`, routing registers
0x68–0x6B), not A–D, which the chipset's own functions (SATA, SMBus, devices 25–31) reach through its DxxIR
registers and which the rotation does not describe. The virt board's device tree gives each INTx its own SPI
already. Shared lines in the kernel (several bindings, every event set, the line unmasked when all acked) and MSI-X
stay for a board that needs them.

**The process registry.** The terminal adds two processes to every boot that has it, the console driver and the
service; with the device manager, two block drivers, the namespace service, `/bin/init`, an application and the
children `Process.Start` makes, the kernel's eight slots (K5.2c) were passed. `WIT_PROCESS_CAPACITY` is twelve in the
release kernel: a slot costs its record (0.35 MiB) and the static kernel stacks of its 32 threads, 64 KiB each with
their guards (2.25 MiB), all in the kernel image, so the release image is 35.5 MiB on x64 with twelve, where sixteen
made 52.5 MiB, which the firmware refuses to load with 128 MiB ("Out of Resources" before the loader runs). The
self-test kernel, 6.5 MiB larger with its test layers and fixtures, keeps eight, since its own tests never hold more
than a few processes and `boot-128` must load. The static arrays per slot stay phase P's question (PLAN.md, phase V).

## Limits kept explicit

- One terminal, one port; no `MULTIPORT`, no second console, no pseudo-terminals (RFC 0020 §27 later).
- No job control: no process groups, no foreground group, `SIGTSTP`, `SIGTTIN` and `SIGTTOU` never raised, `VSUSP`
  ignored; every process that holds the terminal shares its input and receives its signals.
- No `SIGWINCH`: the size changes only when the device reports one, which QEMU's console never does.
- The discipline is the service's; a process cannot bypass it, and `tcsetattr` of one process changes the terminal for
  all, as on Linux.
- The kernel log is not a terminal: a process without one keeps writing there and reading nothing (R4.1).
